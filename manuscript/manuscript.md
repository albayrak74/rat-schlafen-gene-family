%TITLE The rat Schlafen gene family: genomic organization, phylogeny and expression across organs, aging and disease models

%ABSTRACT
Schlafen (SLFN) proteins are interferon-inducible nucleic acid-processing enzymes, but the rat family is poorly characterized and its gene names have changed. We identified the Schlafen genes of the rat GRCr8 assembly and analyzed their structure, phylogeny, constraint and promoters. We examined their expression in eleven organs of Fischer 344 rats at four ages and in six Wistar rat datasets covering colitis, lung injury, adjuvant arthritis, interleukin-1β-exposed microglia, diet-induced obesity and liver aging. The rat genome contains eight annotated Schlafen genes. Seven form a 206-kb cluster on chromosome 10 whose gene order matches the mouse cluster, and Slfnl1 lies on chromosome 5. Rat Slfn9 corresponds to the mouse Slfn8–Slfn9 pair, and the rat and mouse Slfn3 and Slfn4 genes are co-orthologues. Interferon-stimulated response elements were enriched in the eight promoters (9 sites vs 2.5 expected; P = 0.001). Slfn2, Slfn4, Slfn5 and Slfn9 were expressed in all organs, whereas Slfn3 and Slfn14 were barely detected. In aged (104-week) compared with adult (21-week) rats, 21 of 60 gene–organ tests showed higher and 5 lower expression; in several organs these differences paralleled higher expression of the leukocyte marker Ptprc. In Wistar colitis, Slfn4 was 16-fold higher in inflamed than in uninflamed mucosa of the same animals, but no gene was higher after lipopolysaccharide lung injury. A four-gene Schlafen classifier separated aged from adult organs (area under the curve 0.71) but did not outperform random gene sets (P = 0.08). Rat Schlafen expression thus differs by organ, age group and model.

# 1. Introduction

The Schlafen genes were first described in the mouse as a family whose expression changed during thymocyte maturation, and forced expression of Slfn1 slowed the growth of fibroblasts and T cells [@Schwarz1998]. The name, from the German word for "to sleep", refers to this growth-arrest phenotype. Later work in mice showed that Slfn1 restrains cyclin D1 induction [@Brady2005], that other family members modulate T-cell development and activation [@Geserick2004], and that a point mutation in Slfn2 disrupts the quiescence of T cells and monocytes and leads to immunodeficiency [@Berger2010]. Schlafen genes are present in most mammals and in some poxviruses, their copy number varies widely between lineages, and rodents carry the largest known repertoires [@Bustos2009]. Such lineage-specific gains and losses are typical of immune gene families that evolve by repeated duplication and loss [@Nei2005].

Mammalian Schlafen proteins share an N-terminal core composed of an SLFN-specific domain and a divergent AlbA_2 domain, which together form a ribonuclease [@Yang2018; @Metzner2022a; @Jo2022]. Members are traditionally assigned to three groups by length. Group I proteins (mouse Slfn1 and Slfn2) consist of little more than this core. Group II proteins (mouse Slfn3 and Slfn4) add a linker region with a GTPase-like fold. Group III proteins (mouse Slfn5, Slfn8, Slfn9, Slfn10 and Slfn14) also carry a C-terminal helicase domain and, in most cases, a nuclear localization signal [@Geserick2004; @Bustos2009; @Neumann2008]. Human SLFN11 is the best-studied member. It restricts human immunodeficiency virus protein synthesis in a codon-usage-dependent manner [@Li2012], sensitizes cancer cells to DNA-damaging agents [@Zoppoli2012; @Murai2019; @Zhang2021], blocks stressed replication forks [@Murai2018] and cleaves type II transfer RNAs after DNA damage [@Li2018], and its expression is silenced in chemoresistant small cell lung cancer [@Gardner2017]. Human SLFN5 binds transfer RNA and single-stranded nucleic acids without detectable nuclease activity [@Metzner2022b]. It acts as a restriction factor for herpes simplex virus [@Kim2021], represses transcription driven by signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) in glioblastoma [@Arslan2017], and affects the invasiveness of melanoma and renal carcinoma cells [@Katsoulidis2010; @Sassano2015]. SLFN12 cleaves transfer RNA when it is recruited by phosphodiesterase 3A in the presence of velcrin compounds [@Garvie2021; @Lee2023]. SLFN14 is a ribosome-associated endoribonuclease [@Fletcher2018] with antiviral activity [@Seong2017], and its mutations cause an inherited platelet disorder in humans [@Fletcher2015; @VerDonck2023], whereas a corresponding mutation in mice produced species-specific platelet and erythroid phenotypes [@Stapley2021].

Most Schlafen genes are induced by type I interferon (IFN), and several respond to Toll-like receptor ligands in myeloid cells [@Katsoulidis2009; @Mavrommatis2013; @Puck2015]. They belong to the broad class of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), whose promoters typically contain IFN-stimulated response elements (ISREs) or gamma-activated sequences (GAS) bound by STAT and IFN regulatory factor (IRF) proteins [@Darnell1994; @Schneider2014; @Schoggins2019]. Mouse Slfn4 marks activated macrophages [@vanZuylen2011] and myeloid-derived suppressor cells that appear during Helicobacter-induced gastric metaplasia [@Ding2016; @Ding2022], and its deletion reduced lesion formation in a mouse model of atherosclerosis [@Zheng2021]. Schlafen proteins have also been linked to epithelial differentiation in the gut. Rat Slfn3 promotes differentiation of intestinal epithelial cells and has been associated with changes in colonic mucosal growth during aging [@Patel2009a; @Patel2009b], and mouse Slfn3 deletion impairs intestinal adaptation after resection in a sex-dependent way [@VomhofDeKrey2021]. In humans, SLFN12 drives enterocyte differentiation [@Basson2018], SLFN11 has been proposed as a target for mucosal regeneration in ulcerative colitis [@Watanabe2021], and SLFN5 expression has been related to gastric intestinal metaplasia and gastric cancer [@Companioni2017; @Xu2022].

Aging is accompanied by a low-grade, chronic inflammatory state, often termed inflammaging, and transcriptomic surveys of aging mice and rats repeatedly report higher expression of immune and IFN-related genes in many organs of old animals [@Franceschi2018; @Benayoun2019; @Shavlakadze2019; @Schaum2020; @LopezOtin2023]. Activation of the cGAS–STING pathway, which induces type I IFN, has been implicated in this age-related inflammation [@Gulen2023]. Because Schlafen genes are IFN-inducible and expressed in immune and epithelial tissues, their expression may differ between age groups as well as with acute inflammation. We are not aware of a study that has examined the whole Schlafen family across organs and ages in a rodent.

The rat is the standard model for many inflammatory and metabolic disease paradigms, and a multi-organ RNA-sequencing atlas covering four ages is available for the Fischer 344 strain [@Yu2014]. The rat Schlafen family itself has received little systematic attention, and its gene symbols have been reassigned between genome assemblies and annotation releases [@deJong2022], which complicates comparisons between published rat data and current gene models. We therefore set out to (i) define the Schlafen genes of the current rat reference assembly and reconcile their names with earlier annotations, (ii) describe their genomic organization, domain architecture, phylogeny, selective constraint and promoter motifs, (iii) characterize their expression across eleven organs and four ages, and (iv) examine their expression in six Wistar rat datasets on inflammation, obesity and liver aging. Finally, we asked whether Schlafen expression distinguishes aged from adult organs, or inflamed from control tissue, in cross-validated machine-learning models better than random gene sets of the same size.

# 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Genome data and identification of Schlafen genes

Gene models, protein sequences and coding sequences of the rat reference assembly GRCr8 (RefSeq annotation GCF_036323735.1) were downloaded from NCBI [@OLeary2016]. Mouse (GRCm39) and human (GRCh38) proteomes, coding sequences and gene coordinates were obtained from Ensembl [@Harrison2024]; Ensembl and RefSeq use the same GRCr8 assembly coordinates. RefSeq was used as the primary rat annotation because the current Ensembl rat gene set lacks a separate model for Slfn3.

Candidate genes were first taken from the RefSeq annotation (gene symbols beginning with Slfn) and then checked for completeness in three ways. First, the whole RefSeq proteome (all isoforms) was searched with the Pfam profile hidden Markov models of the five families that make up Schlafen proteins [@Mistry2021]: SLFN N-terminal domain (B3R; PF17057), AlbA_2 (PF04326), SLFN GTPase-like domain (PF21026), SLFN group 3 helicase domain (PF09848) and UvrD_C_2 (PF13538). These searches used PyHMMER 0.12.3, a Python interface to HMMER3 [@Eddy2011; @Larralde2023], with the Pfam gathering thresholds. Second, because gathering thresholds can miss divergent domains, family-specific profiles were built from the Pfam-annotated segments of the mouse and human Schlafen proteins (aligned with MAFFT) and searched against the rat proteins (E < 10⁻³). Third, all mouse and human Schlafen proteins were compared with the genomic sequence of the rat cluster region (chromosome 10: 68.2–68.8 Mb) by tblastn (E < 10⁻⁵) to detect coding segments outside the annotated gene models. For each gene, a representative curated transcript was chosen that encodes the longest curated protein; when several transcripts encoded the same protein, the one whose start site was shared by most annotated transcripts was used. Molecular weight, isoelectric point, instability index, grand average of hydropathy and aliphatic index were computed with Biopython [@Cock2009] following the ExPASy ProtParam definitions [@Gasteiger2005; @Guruprasad1990; @Kyte1982; @Ikai1980]. To reconcile names, NCBI GeneIDs, RefSeq accessions and chromosomal coordinates were compared with the gene symbols used in the expression datasets (Section 2.5).

## 2.2. Phylogeny, synteny and selective constraint

The rat, mouse and human Schlafen proteins (24 sequences) were aligned with MAFFT v7 in L-INS-i mode [@Katoh2013]. Columns with gaps in more than half of the sequences were removed, leaving 614 positions. A maximum-likelihood tree was inferred with IQ-TREE 2 [@Minh2020] using ModelFinder [@Kalyaanamoorthy2017] with the Bayesian information criterion, which selected JTT+F+G4, and branch support was estimated with 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (UFBoot) [@Hoang2018]. The tree was rooted on human SLFNL1, the most divergent family member, which lies outside the Schlafen cluster in all three species and shares only the AlbA_2 domain with the other proteins; the root position does not affect the relationships among the clustered genes. Synteny was assessed by comparing the order and orientation of the genes on rat chromosome 10, mouse chromosome 11 and human chromosome 17. Coding sequences of rat–mouse gene pairs at corresponding positions in the two clusters, of rat paralogue pairs and of the mouse Slfn3/Slfn4 and Slfn8/Slfn9 pairs were aligned codon by codon on the basis of their protein alignment, and non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates were estimated with the Nei–Gojobori method [@Nei1986] in Biopython. Ks values above 1.0 were considered saturated and were not interpreted. Pairwise whole-gene ratios were used as a summary of overall constraint; they do not test for selection at individual sites.

## 2.3. Promoter motif analysis

For each rat gene, the 2-kb sequence upstream of the transcription start site of the representative transcript was retrieved from Ensembl. Both strands were scanned for the ISRE (GAAANNGAAA), GAS (TTCNNNGAA), IRF element (IRF-E; AANNGAAA) and nuclear factor κB (NF-κB; GGGRNTYYCC) consensus sequences. Because the IRF-E consensus is contained within the ISRE, these two counts are not independent. As a background, the same motifs were counted in 200 randomly placed 2-kb windows on rat chromosome 10. Enrichment was tested by comparing the total count over the eight promoters with the distribution of totals in 100,000 random draws of eight background windows, and with a Poisson test using the background mean; P values were also Bonferroni-corrected for the four motifs.

## 2.4. Multi-organ expression atlas

The rat BodyMap dataset (accession GSE53960 in the Gene Expression Omnibus, GEO) contains RNA-sequencing data from eleven organs (adrenal gland, brain, heart, kidney, liver, lung, skeletal muscle, spleen, thymus, testis and uterus) of male and female Fischer 344 rats at 2, 6, 21 and 104 weeks of age, with four animals per sex, organ and age (320 samples; Table 1) [@Yu2014; @Barrett2013]. Read counts were obtained from GEO. Because this dataset was annotated with an earlier gene catalogue, gene symbols were mapped to the current names as described in Section 3.1. Expression was summarized as log2(counts per million [CPM] + 1). Tissue specificity was quantified with the τ index, which ranges from 0 for uniform expression to 1 for expression in a single organ [@Yanai2005; @KryuchkovaMostacci2017]. Differential expression between 104-week-old and 21-week-old animals was tested within each organ with PyDESeq2 [@Love2014; @Muzellec2023], with sex as a covariate where both sexes were available. Sex differences were tested in the same samples with age as a covariate, for the nine organs present in both sexes. Within each organ, genes with at least 10 reads in fewer than four samples were considered not expressed and were not tested, and P values withheld by the DESeq2 outlier filter were left missing. P values were corrected with the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) [@Benjamini1995] in two ways: across all Schlafen gene–organ tests of each comparison (family-wise scope, used in the figures) and across all genes of the transcriptome within each organ (transcriptome-wide scope). To examine whether age differences could reflect differences in leukocyte content, the age effect on log2(CPM + 1) of each broadly expressed Schlafen gene was also estimated by linear regression within each organ, with and without adjustment for expression of the pan-leukocyte marker Ptprc (CD45).

## 2.5. Wistar rat datasets

Six Wistar rat datasets were selected from GEO (Table 1). They covered colitis induced with 2,4,6-trinitrobenzene sulfonic acid (TNBS) in colon (GSE149517) [@Boros2020], acute lung injury induced with intratracheal lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (GSE228906), adjuvant-induced arthritis in liver (GSE261056), microglia isolated from neonatal rats exposed to interleukin-1β (IL-1β) (GSE298271) [@Bokobza2025], liver of diet-induced obese rats (GSE192425) [@MetznerV2022], and liver of male and female rats at 110 and 650 days of age (GSE160153) [@LomasSoria2021]. Only control groups and untreated disease groups were used; groups that received experimental therapies were excluded. For GSE192425, only the first sequencing batch, which contained the lean controls, was used, and the saline-treated and sham-operated obese groups were combined. When count data were available, differential expression was tested with PyDESeq2 after removing genes with at least 10 reads in fewer than three samples. Animal was included as a blocking factor for the paired comparison of inflamed and uninflamed colon segments, and developmental stage and sequencing batch for the microglia data. For GSE228906, only FPKM values were provided, and groups were compared with Welch's t-test on log2(FPKM + 1). For GSE160153, normalized values were analyzed with linear models that included age group and sex. FDR was computed across the Schlafen genes that passed the expression filter in each comparison, and transcriptome-wide adjusted P values are also reported (Table S5). Gene symbols were mapped to current names through Ensembl gene identifiers (GSE228906, GSE261056 and GSE298271) or, for datasets that reported only symbols, through the symbol history of the corresponding NCBI GeneIDs (GSE149517 and GSE160153: Slfn3 to Slfn4 and Slfn13 to Slfn9; GSE192425: Slfn13 to Slfn9).

## 2.6. Machine-learning analyses

Two classification tasks were defined. In the aging task, the 160 BodyMap samples from 21-week-old and 104-week-old rats were labeled as adult or aged. Expression values were standardized (z-scores) within each organ so that models could not use differences between organs, and the analysis was restricted to the 12,034 genes with mean log2(CPM + 1) above 0.5 in every organ. In the inflammation task, samples from the four inflammation datasets (TNBS colitis, LPS lung injury, adjuvant arthritis and IL-1β microglia; 68 samples) were labeled as inflamed or not inflamed, with expression standardized within each dataset (and within each batch for the microglia data). In the colitis dataset, the uninflamed segments of TNBS-treated animals were grouped with the control samples. The Schlafen feature set comprised the four genes expressed in all organs and datasets (Slfn2, Slfn4, Slfn5 and Slfn9). For comparison, we used four canonical ISGs, taken as the first four genes of a predefined list (Irf7, Isg15, Mx1, Stat1, Rsad2) present in the gene universe of each task (Irf7, Mx1, Rsad2 and Stat1 for aging; Irf7, Isg15, Mx1 and Rsad2 for inflammation), and the 2,000 genes with the highest variance within organs (aging) or within datasets (inflammation), ranked on centered but unscaled expression values.

Elastic-net logistic regression [@Zou2005] and random forests [@Breiman2001] were fitted with scikit-learn using fixed, untuned hyperparameters (elastic net: mixing parameter 0.5, inverse regularization strength 0.5; random forest: 300 trees, minimum of two samples per leaf). Performance was estimated by leaving out one organ (aging task) or one dataset (inflammation task) at a time, so that each test set came from a tissue or experiment not seen during training. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was computed for each held-out group and for the pooled out-of-group predictions, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) for the pooled AUC from 1000 bootstrap resamples. To judge whether the Schlafen genes performed better than expected by chance, the procedure was repeated for 200 random sets of four genes drawn from the same gene universe, and an empirical P value was computed as (k + 1)/(200 + 1), where k is the number of random sets that reached at least the observed AUC. As a sensitivity analysis, the aging task was repeated without testis. Gene contributions were summarized with Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) values from a random forest of 500 trees fitted to all samples [@Lundberg2020]; these in-sample values describe the fitted model rather than its predictive performance. Finally, an elastic-net model using the three Schlafen genes available in the Wistar liver aging dataset (Slfn2, Slfn4 and Slfn9) was fitted to the BodyMap data and applied to GSE160153. Because small test sets give unstable AUC estimates with wide error margins [@Varoquaux2018], per-group AUC values are reported as descriptive.

## 2.7. Software and reproducibility

Analyses were performed in Python 3.13 with pandas, NumPy, SciPy, statsmodels, scikit-learn, shap, PyDESeq2, Biopython and PyHMMER, and with MAFFT, IQ-TREE 2 and BLAST+; figures were drawn with matplotlib. All random procedures used fixed seeds.

# 3. Results

## 3.1. The rat genome contains eight annotated Schlafen genes with a history of renaming

The RefSeq annotation of GRCr8 contains eight Schlafen genes (Table 2). Seven of them, Slfn5, Slfn9, Slfn2, Slfn1, Slfn4, Slfn3 and Slfn14, lie in a 206-kb cluster on chromosome 10, and the eighth, Slfnl1, lies on chromosome 5. The proteome-wide Pfam search of 97,483 RefSeq proteins detected Schlafen domains in 46, all of which were isoforms encoded by these eight genes (Table S1). The tblastn search of the cluster region detected coding similarity only within the eight gene models, with one exception: a segment of about 200 codons located 2 kb downstream of Slfn3, similar to the C-terminal part of mouse Slfn4 (residues 393–597; 56% identity), with no annotated gene model. It may be a pseudogene fragment or an unannotated exon. The encoded proteins range from 338 amino acids (Slfn1) to 907 amino acids (Slfn9), with predicted molecular weights of 38–104 kDa. Slfn5 had the lowest isoelectric point (6.22), and those of the other proteins ranged from 7.06 to 8.62. All eight proteins are hydrophilic (grand average of hydropathy −0.21 to −0.38), and only Slfn5 had an instability index below 40 (Table 2).

Comparison with earlier annotations showed that three rat genes have carried different names. The gene now named Slfn4 (GeneID 114247) was previously annotated as Slfn3 (RefSeq NM_053687). The gene now named Slfn9 (GeneID 303378) has been labeled Slfn13 or Slfn8 in earlier releases, and its RefSeq proteins are still named "schlafen family member 13". The gene now named Slfn14 (GeneID 287569) appeared as LOC287569. Protein product names also differ from gene symbols for two genes: the Slfn2 protein is named "schlafen family member 12-like" and the Slfn3 protein "schlafen 4". The BodyMap data, which were annotated with an earlier gene catalogue, use the older symbols: their Slfn3, Slfn8 and LOC287569 correspond to current Slfn4, Slfn9 and Slfn14, and their Slfn4 to current Slfn3. The assignment of the BodyMap Slfn3 and Slfn4 symbols was supported by expression. In a liver dataset annotated with current symbols (GSE192425), Slfn4 was about 26 times more abundant than Slfn3 (mean normalized counts 1232 and 47), and in BodyMap liver the transcript labeled Slfn3 was likewise far more abundant than the one labeled Slfn4. All expression results below use the current names.

## 3.2. Conserved gene order and domain architecture

The order and orientation of the seven clustered genes are the same in rat and mouse, except that the mouse cluster contains two genes, Slfn8 and Slfn9, at the position occupied by the single rat Slfn9 (Fig. 1). The human cluster on chromosome 17 contains six genes; instead of direct counterparts of the rodent Slfn1–Slfn4 genes it carries SLFN12 and SLFN12L, which group with them in the tree (Fig. 2a). In all three species, SLFNL1 lies outside the cluster.

In the maximum-likelihood tree, rat Slfn1, Slfn2, Slfn5, Slfn14 and Slfnl1 each formed a well-supported clade with the mouse gene of the same name (UFBoot 97–100; Fig. 2a). Rat Slfn9 was sister to the pair of mouse Slfn8 and Slfn9 (UFBoot 100), which indicates that the duplication producing mouse Slfn8 and Slfn9 took place in the mouse lineage after the split from rat. Consistent with this, Ks between mouse Slfn8 and Slfn9 (0.10) was lower than Ks between rat and mouse genes at corresponding positions (0.19–0.32; Table S3). This rodent clade grouped with human SLFN11 and SLFN13 (UFBoot 87). In contrast, rat Slfn3 and Slfn4 were each other's closest relatives (UFBoot 100), as were mouse Slfn3 and Slfn4. The rat and mouse genes named Slfn3 and Slfn4 are therefore co-orthologues rather than one-to-one orthologues: either the two genes were duplicated independently in each lineage, or they have been homogenized by gene conversion since the split. The Ks values of the rat (0.14) and mouse (0.15) Slfn3/Slfn4 pairs, which are lower than those between the rat and mouse genes (0.25–0.32), fit either explanation. The rodent Slfn1–Slfn4 genes formed a single clade that was sister to human SLFN12 and SLFN12L (UFBoot 98); within it, Slfn2 was closer to Slfn3 and Slfn4 than to Slfn1 (UFBoot 97), so the length-based group I is not monophyletic.

The domain content of the rat proteins matched the length-based groups (Fig. 2b; Table S1). With Pfam gathering thresholds, Slfn1 and Slfn2 contain only the N-terminal domain and AlbA_2, Slfn3 and Slfn4 additionally contain the GTPase-like domain, and Slfn5 and Slfn9 contain all three core domains plus the group 3 helicase domain. Slfn14 contains the N-terminal domain, AlbA_2 and the GTPase-like domain, but its helicase region was not detected at the Pfam gathering threshold, in rat as in mouse and human. The family-specific profiles did detect a group 3 helicase-like region in rat Slfn14 (residues 578–707; E = 8 × 10⁻⁴⁷) and a UvrD_C-like segment near its C terminus, as well as UvrD_C-like segments in Slfn5 and Slfn9. Slfnl1 contains only the AlbA_2 domain.

Gene structures differed in parallel with domain content (Fig. 3a). Slfn1 and Slfn2 have two exons and span less than 7 kb, whereas Slfn3 and Slfn4 have six and eight exons, respectively, and span 23–42 kb, mostly because long introns separate non-coding 5′ exons from the coding region. The representative Slfn9 transcript has seven exons and spans 16 kb; two predicted Slfn9 transcripts start about 30 kb further upstream, within the Slfn2 gene, and were not used.

## 3.3. Promoters are enriched for interferon-stimulated response elements, and coding sequences are constrained

ISRE motifs were found in six of the eight promoters, with nine sites in total against 2.52 expected from the genomic background (resampling P = 0.001; Poisson P = 0.001; P = 0.005 after Bonferroni correction for four motifs; Fig. 3b; Table S2). Slfn4, Slfn5 and Slfn9 each had two ISREs. GAS, IRF-E and NF-κB motifs were not more frequent than in the background (P = 0.47, 0.20 and 0.66, respectively).

All Ka/Ks ratios between rat and mouse genes at corresponding cluster positions were below 1 (Fig. 3c; Table S3), so non-synonymous changes have accumulated more slowly than synonymous ones over the coding sequence as a whole. The ratios were lowest for Slfnl1 (0.16) and Slfn14 (0.20) and highest for Slfn3 (0.60) and Slfn4 (0.64). The paralogue pair Slfn3/Slfn4 had a Ka/Ks of 0.53, and Slfn1/Slfn2 had 0.47. For the older paralogue pairs within group III, synonymous sites were saturated.

## 3.4. Expression across organs and age groups

In the BodyMap data, Slfn4 and Slfn5 were the most abundant family members, followed by Slfn9 and Slfn2, and these four genes were detected in all eleven organs (Fig. 4a). Their expression was highest in spleen, thymus, uterus and lung and lowest in brain, skeletal muscle and testis. Their τ values (0.30–0.54) indicate broad expression. Slfn1 was detected mainly in spleen and thymus (τ = 0.82). Slfnl1 was expressed at moderate levels in most organs, with its highest level in testis (τ = 0.64). Slfn3 and Slfn14 were close to the detection limit in every organ, so their τ values are not informative.

Averaged over organs, Slfn2, Slfn4, Slfn5 and Slfn9 expression was higher at 104 weeks than at 21 weeks, and Slfn2 and Slfn4 expression was also higher at 6 weeks than at 2 weeks (Fig. 4b). Of 61 expressed gene–organ pairs, 60 could be tested. In 26 of these, expression differed between 104-week-old and 21-week-old rats at a family-wise FDR below 0.05, with 21 higher and 5 lower in the aged group (Fig. 5a; Table S4); 17 of the 26 also passed the transcriptome-wide FDR threshold. Slfn4 was higher in aged lung (log2 fold change 1.83), kidney (1.24), spleen (1.18), adrenal gland (1.06), heart (0.73) and thymus (0.48), and Slfn9 in aged lung (1.44), kidney (1.17), brain (1.09), liver (1.07) and adrenal gland (0.37) (Fig. 4c, d). Slfn2 was higher in aged kidney, lung and heart, Slfn5 in aged kidney and thymus (log2 fold changes 0.39–0.95), and Slfnl1 in aged lung (1.01). The largest differences were in testis, where Slfn2, Slfn4, Slfn5 and Slfn9 were 7- to 12-fold higher at 104 weeks, whereas Slfnl1 was 31-fold lower (log2 fold change −4.97; Fig. 4e). Testis Slfnl1 expression was higher at 6 and 21 weeks than at 2 and 104 weeks. Outside testis, lower values in aged animals were found in spleen (Slfn1, −0.97; Slfn2, −0.32), thymus (Slfn2, −0.30) and uterus (Slfn4, −1.44). None of the 49 sex comparisons reached FDR < 0.05 (Fig. 5b).

The pan-leukocyte marker Ptprc was also higher in aged adrenal gland, kidney, liver and lung (log2 differences 0.76–1.35) and in testis (3.34), but lower in aged spleen, thymus and uterus. After adjustment for Ptprc, the age differences in kidney, lung, adrenal gland and liver were no longer significant, whereas those of Slfn9 in brain, Slfn2 in heart, Slfn4 in spleen, Slfn5 in thymus and Slfn2 and Slfn5 in testis remained (Table S4).

## 3.5. Expression in Wistar rat models

The response of Schlafen genes to inflammation differed strongly between models (Fig. 6; Table S5). In TNBS colitis, inflamed segments showed higher Slfn1 (log2 fold change 3.01), Slfn4 (3.96) and Slfn9 (0.75) than uninflamed segments from the same animals (all FDR ≤ 0.002), and the same three genes were higher than in the two control animals (Slfn4, 4.40; Slfn1, 1.54; Slfn9, 1.26; FDR ≤ 0.001). Uninflamed segments did not differ significantly from controls. In liver from rats with adjuvant arthritis, Slfn9 (1.16) and Slfn5 (0.90) were higher than in controls (family-wise FDR = 0.039 for both; transcriptome-wide FDR 0.10 and 0.15). In microglia from IL-1β-exposed neonatal rats, Slfn9 tended to be higher (0.55, FDR = 0.054), whereas Slfn4 was lower without reaching nominal significance (−1.14, P = 0.15). After LPS-induced lung injury no Schlafen gene was higher, and Slfn4 tended to be lower (−1.09, nominal P = 0.026, FDR = 0.13).

Liver from diet-induced obese rats did not differ from lean controls in any Schlafen gene. In the liver aging dataset, expression at 650 days did not differ from that at 110 days, but Slfn4 (−0.97) and Slfn2 (−0.69) tended to be lower in males than in females after adjustment for age (nominal P = 0.026 and 0.027, FDR = 0.055).

## 3.6. Predictive value of Schlafen expression for age group and inflammation

In leave-one-organ-out validation, the elastic-net model built on Slfn2, Slfn4, Slfn5 and Slfn9 separated aged from adult samples with a pooled AUC of 0.71 (95% CI 0.63–0.79; Fig. 7a; Table S6), and the random forest gave a similar value (0.70). Performance varied widely between held-out organs: the AUC was at least 0.90 in testis, lung, kidney and adrenal gland but below 0.50 in skeletal muscle, thymus and uterus. Four canonical ISGs reached a pooled AUC of 0.75, and the 2,000 high-variance genes reached 0.98. The Schlafen model ranked above the median of 200 random four-gene sets (0.55) but not above their 95th percentile (0.72); 15 of the 200 random sets reached at least the observed AUC (P = 0.080; Fig. 7b). Without testis, the pooled AUC was 0.68 (random-set median 0.53; P = 0.070). In the SHAP analysis, Slfn4 contributed most to the age predictions, followed by Slfn9, and higher expression of both genes was associated with predictions of the aged class, whereas Slfn5 showed no consistent direction (Fig. 7c). A three-gene model fitted to the BodyMap data did not separate 650-day-old from 110-day-old Wistar rats in the liver aging dataset (AUC 0.45).

In leave-one-dataset-out validation of the inflammation task, the Schlafen model did not separate inflamed from non-inflamed samples (pooled AUC 0.49 for both the elastic net and the random forest; random-set median 0.50; P = 0.58; Fig. 7d; Table S7). The ISG set (0.37) and the transcriptome-based model (0.59) also transferred poorly between datasets. The AUC for the held-out colitis dataset was low (0.19), although Slfn4 and Slfn9 alone separated inflamed from non-inflamed colon samples perfectly within that dataset (univariate AUC 1.00). This is likely because the models were trained on the other three datasets, in which Slfn4 was unchanged or lower in the inflamed group.

# 4. Discussion

This study provides an inventory of the rat Schlafen family on the current reference assembly, reconciles its gene names with earlier annotations, and describes its expression across organs, age groups and six Wistar disease datasets. The rat cluster has the same gene order as the mouse cluster but a single Slfn9 gene in place of mouse Slfn8 and Slfn9, and its Slfn3 and Slfn4 genes are co-orthologues of their mouse namesakes. The Schlafen promoters are enriched for ISREs, and the broadly expressed members are more highly expressed in aged than in adult rats in several organs, in part in parallel with a marker of leukocyte content. Expression in inflammation differed between datasets, with large increases in colitis, modest increases in arthritic liver and none after LPS lung injury. In cross-validated models, Schlafen expression carried an age-group signal of about the same size as a small ISG set, but this signal did not exceed that of random gene sets and did not separate age groups in an independent Wistar liver dataset.

## 4.1. Evolution and nomenclature of the rat Schlafen genes

The overall structure of the rat family fits the model of rodent Schlafen evolution proposed by Bustos et al. [-@Bustos2009], in which group III genes are shared across mammals while the shorter group I and group II genes have expanded in rodents. The pairing of Slfn3 with Slfn4 within each rodent, together with the low Ks of both paralogue pairs, indicates that these genes either duplicated separately in mouse and rat or have been homogenized by gene conversion since the split. Distinguishing these scenarios will require sequences from additional rodents and formal tests for gene conversion. The mouse-specific duplication that produced Slfn8 and Slfn9 is another example of the lineage-specific gains and losses expected under birth-and-death evolution of immune gene families [@Nei2005]. Whole-gene Ka/Ks ratios between rat and mouse were below 1 for every gene pair but were highest for Slfn3 and Slfn4. This pattern is compatible with relaxed constraint or with positive selection at a minority of sites; pairwise ratios cannot separate these possibilities, and site-based tests on a wider range of species would be needed. The low ratios for Slfnl1 and Slfn14 suggest that these genes have conserved functions, which for SLFN14 is consistent with its role in ribosome-associated RNA cleavage and platelet formation [@Fletcher2015; @Fletcher2018; @VerDonck2023].

SLFN14 has been placed in group III on the basis of its length and position in the cluster [@Bustos2009; @Jo2022]. Its helicase region was missed by the Pfam model at the gathering threshold in all three species but was detected by a profile built from mammalian Schlafen sequences. The C-terminal region of SLFN14 therefore appears to retain a divergent helicase-like domain rather than to have lost it, and automated domain annotations based on gathering thresholds may under-report this domain. Whether the region binds or hydrolyses nucleotides is not known.

The renaming of rat Schlafen genes matters when earlier rat studies are compared with current data. The rat gene studied as Slfn3 in work on intestinal differentiation and colonic aging [@Patel2009a; @Patel2009b] predates the current annotation, in which the RefSeq record previously named Slfn3 (NM_053687, GeneID 114247) belongs to the gene now named Slfn4. Likewise, the rat Slfn13 protein whose crystal structure first revealed the ribonuclease activity of the Schlafen core [@Yang2018] carries the product name of the current Slfn9 locus. We did not have access to the sequences or primers used in those studies, so we cannot confirm which current gene they measured; studies of rat Schlafen genes should report accession numbers alongside gene symbols.

## 4.2. Interferon response elements and age-related expression

The enrichment of ISREs in the Schlafen promoters agrees with the induction of the family by type I IFN in mouse and human cells [@Katsoulidis2009; @Mavrommatis2013; @Puck2015]. The motif analysis is based on consensus matches in 2-kb windows of eight promoters and on a genomic rather than a promoter-matched background, so it describes a sequence feature rather than demonstrating regulation. It is consistent, however, with a link between the family and the IFN-related transcriptional programs frequently observed in aging rodent tissues [@Benayoun2019; @Shavlakadze2019; @Schaum2020].

Higher expression of Slfn4 and Slfn9 in aged kidney, lung and adrenal gland, and of Slfn9 in aged liver and brain, agrees with reports of higher immune and IFN-related gene expression in aged rat and mouse organs [@Benayoun2019; @Shavlakadze2019] and with the inflammaging concept [@Franceschi2018; @LopezOtin2023]. Two explanations are possible. Aged organs may contain more leukocytes such as macrophages, in which Schlafen genes are highly expressed [@vanZuylen2011; @Puck2015], or resident cells may express more Schlafen transcripts in response to chronic IFN signaling, for example through cGAS–STING activation [@Gulen2023]. The Ptprc analysis favors the first explanation in kidney, lung, adrenal gland and liver, where Ptprc was higher in aged animals and adjustment for it removed the age difference. In brain, heart, spleen and thymus, by contrast, some differences remained after adjustment. Because Ptprc is only an approximate marker and adjustment for a correlated variable can over- or under-correct, these results should be confirmed with cell-type deconvolution or single-cell data. The testis results are most simply explained by tissue composition. Lower Slfnl1 expression, whose testis level was highest at 6 and 21 weeks, together with higher expression of the immune-related family members and of Ptprc, is consistent with a smaller proportion of germ cells in the aged testis. The lower Slfn4 expression in aged uterus, the lower Slfn1 expression in aged spleen and the absence of an age difference in Wistar liver show that the age association is not uniform across organs or strains. Because the BodyMap data compare different animals at each age, these are differences between age groups and not changes within individuals.

## 4.3. Differences between inflammation models

The higher expression of Slfn4, Slfn1 and Slfn9 in inflamed colon, but not in uninflamed segments of the same animals, links Schlafen expression to inflamed mucosa in the TNBS model [@Antoniou2016; @Boros2020]. Because rat Slfn4 is a co-orthologue rather than a one-to-one orthologue of mouse Slfn4, mouse findings on Slfn4-expressing myeloid cells [@vanZuylen2011; @Ding2016; @Ding2022] cannot be transferred directly to the rat. The higher colonic expression may nevertheless reflect infiltration of myeloid cells, in line with reports relating SLFN11 to mucosal changes in ulcerative colitis [@Watanabe2021]. The modest increase of Slfn5 and Slfn9 in the liver of arthritic rats, a tissue distant from the joints, may reflect a systemic component of the response, but these effects did not pass the transcriptome-wide FDR threshold.

The absence of an increase after LPS-induced lung injury was unexpected, because LPS induces type I IFN through the Toll-like receptor 4–TRIF pathway [@Yamamoto2003]. The lung samples in GSE228906 were collected at a single time point, and only FPKM values were available, which limits the sensitivity of the comparison. Moreover, the early response of the lung to LPS is dominated by neutrophil recruitment, which may dilute transcripts from resident macrophages. The lower Slfn4 in IL-1β-exposed microglia was not statistically significant. In these six datasets, therefore, Schlafen expression was not consistently higher in inflamed tissue, and the differences depended on tissue, stimulus and sampling.

The lower Slfn4 and Slfn2 in male than in female Wistar liver did not reach the FDR threshold and was not seen in any BodyMap organ. Sex differences in IFN responses are well documented [@Klein2016], and sex-dependent effects of Slfn3 loss have been reported in the mouse intestine [@VomhofDeKrey2021], so this tendency should be examined in a larger liver dataset.

## 4.4. Interpretation of the machine-learning models

The machine-learning analysis asked whether Schlafen expression contains information about age group or inflammation that holds beyond a single organ or experiment. In the aging task, the four-gene model reached an AUC of 0.71 in held-out organs, similar to four canonical ISGs and only slightly lower without testis. Random four-gene sets reached this level in 7.5% of cases, however, and the transcriptome-based model separated the two age groups almost perfectly. The age-group information in Schlafen expression is therefore not specific to the family and adds little to what the transcriptome as a whole provides. The model also failed in Wistar liver, which suggests that the signal learned from Fischer 344 organs depends on strain, age range or tissue. In the inflammation task, none of the feature sets generalized across datasets, which reflects the opposite directions of change in colitis and lung injury. We therefore do not propose Schlafen expression as a marker of age or inflammation in rats; the models serve mainly to quantify how consistent the expression differences are across tissues and experiments.

## 4.5. Limitations

All results come from public transcriptomic data, and no new experiments were performed, so protein levels, cellular localization and enzyme activities were not assessed. Gene identification relies on the GRCr8 RefSeq annotation; the tblastn search found no further complete genes in the cluster, but the unannotated Slfn3/Slfn4-like segment downstream of Slfn3 was not characterized further. The phylogenetic and selection analyses include only three species and use pairwise whole-gene Ka/Ks ratios. The mapping of gene symbols in older datasets is supported by GeneIDs, coordinates and expression patterns, but the BodyMap assignment of Slfn3 and Slfn4 rests on expression evidence rather than on sequence-level reannotation. Promoter motifs were identified by consensus matching without experimental confirmation of binding. The Wistar datasets differ in design, tissue, platform and normalization, and several have small groups (the colitis dataset has only two control animals), so effects that were not significant may still exist and some significant effects may be imprecise. FDR control within the Schlafen family is less stringent than transcriptome-wide control, and both are reported. The BodyMap aging data come from a single inbred strain and compare different animals at each age. Finally, bulk expression differences cannot be attributed to particular cell types, and the Ptprc adjustment is only an approximation of leukocyte content.

# 5. Conclusions

The rat genome contains eight annotated Schlafen genes. Seven form a cluster on chromosome 10 with the same gene order as the mouse cluster; the rat has a single Slfn9 where the mouse has Slfn8 and Slfn9, and the rat and mouse Slfn3 and Slfn4 genes are co-orthologues. Several rat Schlafen genes have changed names between annotation releases, so rat studies should be compared through accession numbers. The promoters are enriched for ISREs, and the broadly expressed members Slfn2, Slfn4, Slfn5 and Slfn9 are expressed at higher levels in aged than in adult rats in several organs, often together with higher leukocyte marker expression. Their expression in inflammation varied between datasets, from large increases in colitis to none in LPS lung injury. In cross-validated models, Schlafen expression carried an age-group signal comparable to that of canonical ISGs but not stronger than that of random gene sets. These cross-sectional associations need to be confirmed at the protein and cellular level, and longitudinal and cell-resolved studies are needed to determine which cell populations account for the age- and inflammation-related differences in Schlafen expression.
